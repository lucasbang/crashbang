In the early 1900s, starting a car required a certain amount of specialized knowledge, manual strength, and courage. The driver would set the air/fuel mixture and adjust the spark timing by hand, walk around to the front of the car, and insert a right-angled crank rod through a hole in the front below the grille. Heaving on the crank with all their might would rotate the crankshaft and move the pistons through the cylinders, pulling in a mixture of air and fuel, which would ignite and then start the engine. If the engine fired at the wrong moment, or backfired, the crank could kick back hard enough to break your wrist. The injury became known as a "chauffeur's fracture" [@andreotti2020chauffeur].

In 1912, Charles Kettering introduced the electric starter, first installed in the Cadillac [@smithsonianDriving]. The crank gave way to a switch that allowed power to flow from a battery to the electric motor connected to the crankshaft. Later, a keyed ignition switch was introduced to control access to starting the engine. Today, many cars start with a single button that hides a complex sequence of events: the car confirms that the key fob is sufficiently nearby, exchanges an encrypted handshake with it to unlock the immobiliser, checks that your foot is on the brake, primes the fuel pump, and then turns the engine over. Each time a new layer of technology has been added, the driver needs to know less about engines and perform less physical effort. 

According to mathematician Alfred North Whitehead: 

> Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking of them. [@whitehead1911introduction]

So, more people have been able to drive cars without thinking about engines. Progress! 

Or is it? Perhaps not, according to French philosopher [Simondon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Simondon). 

## Simondon on concretization and technological opacity

Gilbert Simondon, in his 1958 *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, used automobile hand cranks as an example to complicate the common understanding of technological advancement. He observed that technical objects---automobiles, for example---evolve through a process that he called *concretization* [-@simondon2017mode]. 

Earlier versions of technical objects tend to consist of relatively independent components, where each is designed to perform a particular function with simple interactions between them. As technologies develop, these components become increasingly integrated. Over time, individual components merge into composite structures with multiple mutually supportive functions. This results in a more internally cohesive technical system, whose operation depends much less on external adjustments and regular intervention.

For a car engine's electric starting system, the internal workings have become opaque to the everyday driver. Note that, by Simondon's account, concretization does not necessarily imply increasing opacity, yet the two can often accompany one another. Regardless, as technologies become more integrated and self-regulating, users are able to operate them without needing an understanding of their "concretized" (and often opaque) internal mechanisms. 

This is definitely attractive to users, leading to wider and faster adoption. They can benefit from technologies without needing to study, train, or exert physical effort. Much of that studying, training, and manual effort was already done by somebody else at an earlier time, and is no longer needed to benefit from the use the technology. Extending Simondon's manual hand-crank car ignition system to the modern car ignition system is just a further elaboration of tendency. An increasingly complex, layered, engineered system replaces a process that once required considerable knowledge and skill with the simple press of a single button! 

So far, so good, but now for the complication. Simondon criticized automobile manufacturers for presenting the elimination of the hand crank as an example of "technical progress". Removing the crank simplified the driver's experience, sure, but it also eliminated a backup mechanism and made the vehicle more dependent on its electrical system. By relieving the user of the practice of the mental and physical effort to start the car by hand, drivers are left stranded if the electric starter fails. [Heidegger's concept of *ready-to-hand* [-@heidegger1927being] offers a related perspective. During ordinary habituated use, a tool recedes from conscious attention. We attend to what we are trying to accomplish with it, rather than to the tool itself. When things stop working smoothly, we are forced into a potentially jarring cognitive state called *present-at-hand*. Simondon describes the evolution of technical objects, while Heidegger helps explain how those objects disappear from or demand our attention as we use them. Stay tuned for another log entry on this.]{.margin-note}

What car manufacturers marketed as simplification was, to Simondon, a technical complication: making a technology easier to use is not necessarily the same thing as making it technically better [-@simondon2017mode]. Luckily, there are people who find the inner workings of vehicles and other technologies interesting or profitable enough to understand them and be able to fix them for others when they break.  

In any case, together these ideas suggest a relationship between technological development, opacity, and ubiquity. Roughly speaking:

**Technologies become easier to adopt when people do not need to understand how they work**. 

And they become commonplace when their internal complexity no longer requires careful attention for operation. Perhaps this observation is already obvious, but I think it is worth stating, and Simondon's philosophy of technology helps us think through its nuances, especially with respect to computational technologies.

## Applying the idea to programming

If you are a programmer, the discussion so far probably feels familiar. The above-described pattern appears everywhere in computer science, where *abstraction* separates interface from implementation, hides underlying details, renames hundreds of lines of code as a single function name, or allows a programmer to forget that underneath it all is the carefully orchestrated flow of electrons through sophisticated circuits. [Simondon actually discussed computer programming in 1958, describing programmable computers as possessing a *margin of indeterminacy*: they can perform different operations depending on the program they receive. See [-@simondon2017mode]. I find this interesting in its own right, though it doesn't seem particularly relevant to the discussion here. I'm not aware of Simondon actually applying his concepts of abstraction and concretization to programming, and it would seem natural to me that he would have, but I may just not have found it yet.]{.margin-note}

As software becomes more complex and layered, users of a programming abstraction, whether a function, module, type checker, library, or API, need only understand how to interact with its interface [@parnas1972criteria], not its internally "concretized" algorithms. Encapsulation makes increasingly sophisticated systems available through increasingly simple means. In the same way that we drive a car to get from place to place, and not for the purposes of turning the crankshaft and burning fuel (at least I hope), we use software abstractions to accomplish higher-level goals. We want to sort a list, render an image, or train a model, without necessarily investing too much cognitive effort in the particular algorithms, instructions, or movements of electrons that make it actually happen.

But the programmer's abstraction is not quite what Simondon meant by *concretization*. In fact, I think there is a curious and unfortunate conflict of terminology. Simondon describes technical evolution as a movement from the *abstract* toward the *concrete*, while computer scientists frequently describe progress in programming as the construction of increasingly powerful *abstractions*. I found it quite challenging to read Simondon at first because of my differing programmer's understanding of the words he uses. [To make matters even more confusing, in [*abstract interpretation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_interpretation), concretization refers to the mathematical operation of mapping an abstract description back to the concrete states it represents, something quite different from Simondon's use of the term.]{.margin-note}

For Simondon, an abstract technical object's components remain relatively independent, with each fulfilling a narrowly defined individual function. Concretization occurs as these initially independent components become more integrated and mutually supporting. For computer scientists and programmers, abstraction generally means suppressing implementation details behind simpler representations or interfaces.

These two different senses of abstraction and concretization are not necessarily opposed. In fact, they may reinforce one another. Consider a compiler: its implementation may become increasingly integrated and sophisticated, while the programming language it supports allows programmers to ignore ever more details of the underlying machine. Increasing concretization at the implementation level can support increasing abstraction at the interface level.

There is another interesting difference between the car's ignition button and a programming abstraction. An ignition button conceals complexity while offering essentially one single operation that can't really be arbitrarily combined with other operations. On the other hand, a software library also conceals complexity, but its interface may allow programmers to combine various function to construct things its authors never anticipated. In either case, the implementation is hidden behind an interface, but the possibilities for (re-)use and composition are very different.

As many programmers know, abstraction can simultaneously conceal details and expose possibilities. It makes some aspects of a system totally inaccessible or irrelevant while making other affordances available. Or, said another way, a system can become increasingly opaque at one level while enabling entirely new possibilities at another.

## And now AI

The progression from hand-cranked engines to push-button ignition has an obvious parallel in programming. We figured out how to delegate individual logical operations to circuits [@shannon1940symbolic], which we made smaller and combined into CPUs, created machine instruction languages to control them, invented compilers so we could write higher level programs, and designed increasingly complex computational systems [@nisan2021elements]. [If you want to experience this yourself in a highly entertaining way, I highly recommend the [Nandgame] sequence of puzzles (https://nandgame.com/). Nisan and Schocken's *The Elements of Computing Systems*, better known as [Nand to Tetris](https://www.nand2tetris.org/), walks through the whole progression hands-on. You start from a single NAND gate, build the logic gates, an ALU, a CPU, an assembler, a compiler, and a eventually a simple operating system, then run programs like Tetris on the result! The full course materials are free online.]{.margin-note}

And now, with LLM coding agents, we are beginning to delegate the writing of that software itself. Just as the driver no longer needs to know how to start an engine, the programmer may increasingly accomplish tasks without needing to know how to implement them. (And maybe LLM coding agents will finally do for programmers' repetitive strain wrist injuries what the electric starter did for chauffeur's fracture.)

Large language models offer extraordinarily simple interfaces to enormously complex technical systems. Users can produce software, images, and text without understanding the mechanisms responsible for generating them. In this respect, modern AI systems continue a familiar trajectory of programming abstraction: increasingly concretized machinery becomes available through increasingly simplified interfaces.

Still, there is something slightly different about delegating the work of programming to LLMs. A programmer's abstractions allow them to ignore lower-level details while continuing to retain considerable control over what their programs do. A programmer can use a library without understanding its implementation, but still have a deep understanding of the program they are constructing with it. With LLM-generated code, that understanding may be becoming increasingly optional. We might use programs whose implementation nobody has written or read [@qiao2026comprehension].

Of course, this is not necessarily a bad thing in-and-of itself. Abstraction has always allowed people to accomplish things without understanding every underlying mechanism. And LLMs do enable people who otherwise would never have learned to program to create software. But the increasing gap between *using* a technology and *understanding* it raises some interesting questions, particularly when we consider the push for explainable AI.

Simondon regarded the separation of technical knowledge from ordinary culture as a form of alienation. He was concerned that people increasingly live among technical objects whose operation they do not understand. A technology that anyone can use but few can understand may be extremely successful, while still presenting a problem for the relationship between humans and machines.

## Conclusion

I find it curious that the things we build often become easier to use precisely because we no longer need to understand much about how they work. In programming, we have even made this a central design principle, calling it abstraction. Simondon's account complicates this story by reminding us that a simpler interface (e.g. a push-button electric starter on a car) does not necessarily mean a better technical object. And now, as AI agents become increasingly autonomous and write software themselves, I wonder how far this gap between using a technology and understanding it can go.
